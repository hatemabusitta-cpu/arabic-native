## 1. History and layers of Arabic

### 1.1 Pre-Islamic Arabic: poetry as the first standard

- Before Islam, Arabian tribes spoke related dialects, but **poetry used a shared, elevated language** that all tribes understood. Poets competed at markets such as **سوق عكاظ** (Souq Ukaz, near Taif). The most celebrated odes were later grouped as **المعلقات** (the Mu'allaqat, "the suspended odes"). [common knowledge]
- The classic opening of Imru' al-Qays shows the conventions of that register, the **standing at the abandoned campsite**: «قفا نبكِ من ذكرى حبيبٍ ومنزلِ» ("Halt, let us weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling"). Arabic prestige writing still draws on rhythm, image density and compression from this layer.
- **Why it matters for writers:** Arabic readers react strongly to **sound** (rhythm, rhyme, word music). A line that "sounds right" persuades in a way that a literally accurate line does not. This explains much of the success of saj' slogans (section 5).

### 1.2 The Quran and the standardization of Classical Arabic

- The Quran (7th century) fixed one prestigious variety of Arabic as the reference point for grammar, vocabulary and eloquence (**الفصاحة**). [common knowledge]
- Script reforms followed so that the text could be read correctly by new non-Arab Muslims:
  - The **Uthmanic codex** standardized the written consonantal text.
  - **Dots** (الإعجام), which separate ب ت ث and similar letters, and **vowel marks** (الحركات) were added gradually. Tradition credits أبو الأسود الدؤلي with the early vowel dots. **الخليل بن أحمد الفراهيدي** introduced the vowel signs used today (فتحة، ضمة، كسرة، شدة). [common knowledge]
- Grammar became a science. **سيبويه**'s *الكتاب* (8th century) is the founding grammar. Al-Khalil's **كتاب العين** is the first Arabic dictionary, ordered by sound and organized by roots. [common knowledge]
- **Effect today:** Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) grammar is still essentially Classical grammar. The Quran remains the highest model of style, so **quoting or parodying Quranic phrasing in ads is sensitive** in the Gulf. Avoid it unless the context is religious and respectful.

### 1.3 The Abbasid golden age and the translation movement

- In Baghdad (8th–10th centuries), **بيت الحكمة** (House of Wisdom) and translators such as **حنين بن إسحاق** rendered Greek, Persian, Syriac and Indian science and philosophy into Arabic. [common knowledge]
- Translators used two strategies, and both are still in use today:
  - **Borrowing (التعريب):** فلسفة (philosophy), جغرافيا (geography), موسيقى (music), أسطرلاب (astrolabe).
  - **Native derivation from roots:** جوهر/عرض (substance/accident), علّة (cause), كمّية (quantity, from كم "how much"), كيفية (quality/manner, from كيف "how").
- Arabic in turn lent words to Europe: algebra (الجبر), algorithm (from الخوارزمي al-Khwarizmi), alcohol (الكحل), zero/cipher (صفر), sugar (سكر), coffee (قهوة). This history is useful for brand storytelling.

### 1.4 The Nahda (19th–early 20th century revival) and coining modern vocabulary

- With the printing press, newspapers, and contact with Europe (Egypt under Muhammad Ali, Ottoman Levant), writers had to name trains, telephones and parliaments. Key figures include رفاعة الطهطاوي, بطرس البستاني (encyclopedia and the dictionary *محيط المحيط*) and the اليازجي family. [common knowledge]
- **Revival of old words with new meanings** was the signature Nahda technique, and it still works for brand naming:

| Modern word | Old meaning | New meaning |
|---|---|---|
| قطار | a line of camels tied together | train |
| سيارة | a caravan (Quranic) | car |
| جريدة | stripped palm frond used for writing | newspaper |
| هاتف | an unseen voice that calls out | telephone |
| مجلة | scroll/collection of wisdom | magazine |
| دبابة | a siege engine pushed toward walls | tank |

- **Arabic language academies** were founded to regulate coinage: Damascus (1919), Cairo (1932), Baghdad (1947) and Amman (1976). Rabat hosts the Arabization coordination bureau, and there is a Union of Arab Academies. Their main tools are **derivation (اشتقاق)**, **semantic extension (مجاز)**, **Arabization/borrowing (تعريب)**, **blending (نحت)**, **calquing** and the **revival of classical words**. [verified: USP, Durham, Zenodo sources]
  - Blending (نحت): برمائي (amphibious, from برّ "land" + ماء "water"); رأسمالية (capitalism, from رأس + مال); كهرومغناطيسي (electromagnetic).
  - Compounding with لا: لاسلكي (wireless), لاإرادي (involuntary).
- **The derivation versus borrowing debate continues.** Academies prefer derived terms. Users often prefer the borrowed word. Researchers studying computing terms found that several mechanisms compete for the same concept, which causes inconsistency. [verified: Durham thesis]

| Concept | Academy/derived | What people actually say |
|---|---|---|
| computer | حاسوب | كمبيوتر |
| television | تلفاز | تلفزيون |
| radio | مذياع | راديو |
| mobile phone | هاتف محمول / جوال | جوال (Saudi), موبايل (Egypt, Levant) |
| internet | الشابكة (proposed) | إنترنت |
| bank | مصرف | بنك (both are normal) |

  **Rule for the skill:** use the term **the target audience actually uses** in that context. In formal and government text, use the established Arabic term (حاسوب may appear in education, مصرف in banking law). In consumer copy, use the living word (كمبيوتر, إنترنت). Never invent a "pure" coinage that nobody uses.
- **Saudi Arabia today:** **مجمع الملك سلمان العالمي للغة العربية** (King Salman Global Academy for Arabic Language) was founded in 2020 in Riyadh. It approves and publishes terminology, passes terms to government bodies, and contributes Arabic entries to the UN terminology portal. Recent work includes specialized glossaries such as equestrian terms. [verified: ksaa.gov.sa, Saudipedia, Arab News]
- **Modern coinages built on patterns:** عولمة (globalization, pattern فوعلة), رقمنة (digitization), حوسبة (computerization), أتمتة (automation, borrowed root + Arabic pattern), شخصنة (personalization/taking personally), أنسنة (humanization), تغريدة (tweet, from "bird song"), تطبيق (app, from "application/implementation"), منصة (platform, from "podium").

### 1.5 Modern Standard Arabic (الفصحى المعاصرة)

- MSA is the written standard of news, government, education, books, legal text and most UI. Compared with Classical Arabic, it has:
  - simpler syntax and more SVO sentences
  - a large modern vocabulary
  - heavy influence from English and French phrasing (some natural, some calque)
  - case endings that are mostly unpronounced in speech.
- MSA is **nobody's mother tongue**. Everyone learns it at school. This is why "correct but stiff" text is the most common failure: it is grammatical MSA that no person would say.

### 1.6 Diglossia (الازدواجية اللغوية)

- In 1959 Charles Ferguson described Arabic as **diglossic**: a "High" variety (Fusha) for writing and formal settings, and a "Low" variety (dialect, العامية) for home and daily life. The varieties differ in function, prestige, how they are acquired, and how standardized they are. [verified: Wikipedia, academic sources]
- Later scholars showed that this is really a **continuum**. The Egyptian linguist **السعيد بدوي** (Badawi, 1973) described five levels, from "heritage Classical" (فصحى التراث) through "contemporary Fusha" (فصحى العصر) to "the colloquial of the educated" (عامية المثقفين), "of the enlightened" (عامية المتنورين), and "of the illiterate" (عامية الأميين). [common knowledge]
- **Practical consequence:** most effective modern copy sits in the **middle of the continuum**: simple MSA, or educated dialect without heavy local markers. Examples on the same topic, from most formal to most casual:
  - High MSA: «يسرّنا إعلامكم بأنه قد تمّ تفعيل حسابكم.» ("We are pleased to inform you that your account has been activated.") This is stiff.
  - Light MSA: «فعّلنا حسابك. أهلًا بك!» ("We activated your account. Welcome!")
  - Saudi white dialect: «حسابك جاهز، حيّاك!» ("Your account is ready, welcome!")
  - Strong Najdi: «حسابك جاهز، حيّاك الله ومرحبا ترى كل شي تمام» (natural for some audiences, too local for a national brand).

## 2. Root and pattern (الجذر والوزن)

### 2.1 How it works

- Most Arabic words are built from a **consonantal root** (usually three letters) carrying a core meaning, poured into a **pattern** (وزن) that adds a grammatical or semantic role. Grammarians use the dummy root ف-ع-ل to name patterns. [common knowledge]
- Root **ك-ت-ب** (writing):

| Word | Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| كَتَبَ | فَعَلَ | he wrote |
| كاتِب | فاعِل | writer (agent) |
| مَكتوب | مَفعول | written / a letter (patient) |
| كِتاب | فِعال | book |
| مَكتَب | مَفعَل | office/desk (place) |
| مَكتَبة | مَفعَلة | library/bookshop |
| كُتَيِّب | فُعَيِّل | booklet (diminutive) |
| كاتَبَ | فاعَلَ | corresponded with |
| تَكاتَبَ | تَفاعَلَ | wrote to each other |
| اِستَكتَبَ | اِستَفعَلَ | asked someone to write / dictated |
| كَتيبة | فَعيلة | battalion (semantic drift) |

### 2.2 Productive noun patterns copywriters use

| Pattern | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| فاعِل | doer | سائق (driver), مُطوِّر (developer, from form II) |
| مَفعول | thing done | مَحفوظ (saved), مَطلوب (required/wanted) |
| مَفعَل / مَفعِل / مَفعَلة | place | مَطبخ (kitchen), مَلعب (stadium), مَطار (airport), مَدرسة (school) |
| مِفعال / مِفعَل / مِفعَلة | tool | مِفتاح (key), مِكنسة (broom), مِبرد (file tool) |
| فَعّال | profession, or intensive | خَبّاز (baker), نَجّار (carpenter), طَيّار (pilot) |
| فَعّالة | machine | غسّالة (washing machine), ثلّاجة (fridge), سيّارة (car), طيّارة (plane) |
| فاعِلة | device (modern) | طابِعة (printer), حاسِبة (calculator) |
| فِعالة | craft or trade | تِجارة (trade), زِراعة (farming), صِناعة (industry), خِياطة (tailoring) |
| فُعال | illness | صُداع (headache), زُكام (cold), سُعال (cough) |
| فَعَلان | movement/agitation | طَيَران (flying), غَلَيان (boiling), جَرَيان (flow) |
| فُعْلة / فِعْلة | one instance / manner | ضَربة (a strike), جَلسة (a session/sitting) |

Coining product names with these patterns works because readers instantly decode the role. **جاهز** ("ready", فاعل) is a Saudi delivery app, **مِرسال** ("messenger", مِفعال) is a common business name, and **منصّة** ("platform", place pattern) is the name of the government gateway.

### 2.3 The verb forms (الأوزان / Forms I–X)

| Form | Pattern | Typical meaning | Example (root) | UI/marketing relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | فَعَلَ | base | دَرَسَ studied | |
| II | فَعَّلَ | causative or intensive | دَرَّسَ taught; فَعَّلَ activated; حَمَّلَ downloaded/loaded; خَصَّصَ customized | Many UI verbs: فعّل، حمّل، حدّث، عدّل |
| III | فاعَلَ | doing with someone, attempt | شارَكَ shared; تابَعَ followed; ساعَدَ helped | شارك، تابع، راسلنا |
| IV | أَفعَلَ | causative | أَرسَلَ sent; أَضافَ added; أَنشأَ created; أَكمَلَ completed | Imperative **keeps** hamza: أرسِل، أضِف، أنشِئ، أكمِل، أدخِل |
| V | تَفَعَّلَ | reflexive of II | تَعَلَّمَ learned; تَحَقَّقَ verified | تحقّق من بريدك |
| VI | تَفاعَلَ | mutual | تَعاوَنَ cooperated; تَواصَلَ communicated; تَفاعَلَ interacted | تواصل معنا، تفاعل |
| VII | اِنفَعَلَ | passive/resultative | اِنكسرَ broke; اِنضَمَّ joined | انضم إلينا |
| VIII | اِفتَعَلَ | middle/reflexive | اِشتَرَكَ subscribed; اِختارَ chose; اِنتظرَ waited | اشترك، اختر، انتظر |
| IX | اِفعَلَّ | colors/defects | اِحمَرَّ turned red; اِخضَرَّ turned green | rare |
| X | اِستَفعَلَ | seeking, deeming, using | اِستَخدَمَ used; اِستَعلَمَ inquired; اِستَردَّ got back | استخدم، استرداد المبلغ (refund) |

**Why this matters for natural word choice:**
- **Nuance lives in the form.** أعلَمَ means to inform; عَلَّمَ means to teach. أخبَرَ means to tell; استخبَرَ means to inquire. A translator choosing the wrong form produces odd text. "We'll let you know" is «سنُعلمك» or more simply «سنخبرك», never «سنعلّمك» ("we'll teach you").
- **One root covers a whole feature family**, which keeps UI consistent. Root ش-ر-ك gives شارِك (share, button), مشاركة (sharing, menu), اشترك (subscribe), اشتراك (subscription), مشترك (subscriber), شريك (partner), شركة (company). Choose deliberately: "Share" is شارك or مشاركة, and "Subscribe" is اشترك. Mixing them confuses users.
- **Hamza spelling depends on the form** (see 7.9): form IV imperatives take hamzat al-qat' (أرسِل), while forms VII, VIII and X take hamzat al-wasl with no hamza sign (اختر، استخدم، انضم).
- **Readers understand new coinages they have never seen**, provided the pattern is right. Recent examples include «مُؤثِّر» (influencer, from "to affect"), «مُتابِع» (follower), «تغريدة» (tweet), «مُحتوى» (content), «صانع محتوى» (content creator). A coinage on a wrong or rare pattern feels foreign.
